\section[The Good Regulator Framework for Reinforcement Learning]{The Good Regulator Framework for Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:objective}


The good regulator of \citet{Conant-good70} is a cybernetic take on control that formulates regulation as counteracting arbitrary disturbances to achieve desirable outcomes over a distribution of states (Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot}).
Arguably the paper is best known for its aphorism ``\textbf{every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system}'', which imposes a necessary condition for the simplest optimal regulator to represent the system it regulates.
This modelling condition is a consequence of the surrounding framework, which is a useful theoretical frame that overlaps in some parts, and diverges in others, from the concepts we usually deal with in reinforcement learning (RL).
Therefore, this section adapts the good regulator recipe to modern RL by interpreting the underlying statements of good regulator to explicit RL machinery.
The resulting object is a modelling recipe and optimization objective compatible with convex RL \citep{Zahavy-reward21}, from which the following sections derive an identifiability condition and algorithm to find general policies.
Proofs are in Supp.~\ref{sup:proofs}.

A disturbance $D$ causes events in a system $S$ which a regulator counteracts with an action $A$.
The pair generates an outcome $Z = \psi(S,A)$ in some set $\Zcal$ with a desirable set $G \subset \Zcal$.
A cause-controlled regulator acts on $D$, while an error-controlled regulator acts on the state $S$ caused by $D$, so that by the data processing inequality $I(A;D) \le I(S;D)$.
For a fixed state distribution $p(s)$, the good regulator should keep $Z \in G$, and the criterion asks for minimal entropy in the outcome law $\forall D$,
{\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{4pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{3pt}
\begin{align*}
    \min_{\pi(a \mid s)}\ H(Z), \qquad Z = \psi(S,A), \quad S \sim p(s), \quad A \sim \pi(\cdot \mid S).
\end{align*}}
Since the law of $Z$ is linear in the policy and entropy is concave, an optimal policy is deterministic.
The aphorism ``\textbf{every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system}'' then refers to the argument that in the cause-controlled setting the simplest optimal regulator $\pi^\star$ has a deterministic map $\pi\colon S \to A$, so observing $D$ requires the regulator to factor through the relation $\phi\colon D \to S$, such that $\pi^\star = \pi \circ \phi$.

\paragraph{Mapping the good regulator of Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot} onto RL.}
In the POMDP of \S\ref{sec:prelim} a policy $\pi \in \Pi$ acts on the history, where $\Pi$ is the set of history policies $\pi(a \mid x)$ and $\Pi_b \subset \Pi$ the stationary belief-policies $\pi(a \mid b)$, which under the dynamics $P$ induces the state-action occupancy $\occ$.
We can make a link from the good regulator for the state marginal of $\occ$, which maps cleanly onto the state law $p(S)$ of Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot} through the interpretation of relative long-run frequencies.
Naturally, the notion of regulation coincides with actions and policies.
The main conceptual jump from the good regulator to RL sits in the optimization criterion. 
In typical POMDPs, an optimal policy is defined as maximizing the expected cumulative sum of a reward function $\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{d^\pi} r(s, a)$.
The closest analogy of reward in the good regulator sits in the deterministic outcome channel $z = \psi(s,a)$.
An optimal regulator is then defined by the policy that minimizes the entropy on the outcome law as expressed through the pushforward $p_\pi(z) = \psi_\#\occ$ such that $\min_\pi H[p_\pi(z)]$.
Similar to convex RL from \S\ref{sec:prelim}, this objective cannot be expressed with a fixed reward on state-action pairs.
Moreover, a major caveat with this objective is that minimal entropy alone leads to the dark room problem \citep{Friston-dark12}, where an agent finds a dark room, stands still and predictably accomplishes nothing.
Keeping the outcome in a desirable set $G \subset \Zcal$ resolves this, which \citet{Conant-good70} sketch but then leave implicit.

The last core ingredient from the good regulator to map onto RL is the disturbance and how it leads to the error- and cause-controlled regulation of Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot}.
Like the abstraction of state, disturbances are general, as they can be exogenous or endogenous to the agent and regular or irregular in time.
An exogenous disturbance most closely maps onto error-controlled regulation, as it enters through the dynamics $P$ or the outcome channel $\psi$ and the agent can only counteract its effects.
For instance, it can be irreducible measurement or actuator noise, an occlusion of the state, or a drift in the environment law.
The cause-controlled setting is more subtle, as it counters a disturbance before it enters the state, but whether a disturbance is viewed as entering before or after depends on the boundary of the environment state.
\citet[p.~92]{Conant-good70} illustrate this with a cow that senses cold air at its skin before its blood temperature falls, which can be seen as cause control on the blood temperature but error control on the skin.
Endogenous examples of cause control follow more naturally, since an agent can read its own processes before they take effect, for example by anticipating the interference of its own learning or the sensory changes that its actions will cause in its own embodiment.
The endogenous reading is also what leads to the aphorism of modelling.
A policy that acts directly on the history is error-controlled, yet the agent may instead act on a belief inferred from this history.
This belief predicts the hidden state and the regular disturbances, so from its internal view the policy counters the prediction as if it directly read the cause.
As an example take a legged robot walking on ice, correcting its balance after a slip is error control, choosing its next step from friction it sensed in recent steps is acting on a prediction of the cause, and seeing the ice ahead is reading the cause before it enters the gait.
The simplest optimal policy thus factors through the belief, and therefore the belief must be a model in the sense of \citet{Conant-good70}.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!4, colframe=gray!60, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, left=6pt, right=6pt, top=4pt, bottom=4pt]
\small
\textbf{Takeaway on Models.}
A cause-controlled regulator must model how the disturbance causes the state, and an error-controlled regulator is a cause-controlled regulator on a model of the cause.
The criterion pushes the regulator to act on the simplest model that minimizes the entropy of the outcome law.
\end{tcolorbox}


\paragraph{A Good Regulator Recipe for RL.}
We now turn the criterion and our view of disturbances and models into an RL recipe in two parts.
The first is how the outcome and its entropy criterion align with goal-reaching behaviour through the good set $G \subset \Zcal$.
The second, and more involved, part is what an agent can at best capture of the outcome.
This latter part is determined by the affordances.
% This depends on the causal structure of the outcome law and on the affordances the environment consequently offers the agent.
\begin{definition}[Affordances \citep{Gibson-ecological79, Khetarpal-what20}]\label{def:affordances}
  The affordances of an agent are the effects that its environment permits as a consequence of the agent, which influences the environment strictly through actions produced by an internal mapping from observations over time.
\end{definition}
Affordances thus induce the set of outcome laws that the agent can reach.
The definition is also relative to the boundary that is drawn between agent and environment.
The outcome law imposes a necessary lower bound on the affordances, whereas the affordances impose an upper bound on what the agent can achieve of that outcome.
Between these bounds, the criterion pushes the model down until it just satisfies the law of requisite variety \citep{Ashby-introduction56}.
Thus, an agent with too limited affordances sits below this bound and must coarse-grain the outcome law.
Both parts matter for goal-reaching behaviour because entropy depends on the space it is measured on, which is set by the goal we assign, our agent's affordances and their interplay.
We express goal-seeking through the agent's affordances in the algebra of entropy on measurable spaces, where the pushforward to a coarse-grained law and a change of measure and base give the following theorem.
\begin{theorem}[The good regulator criterion for RL]
\label{thm:criterion}
Let $\nu \ll \lambda$ be a goal and a base measure on $\Zcal$ with $\psi_\# \occ \ll \nu, \forall \pi$, and let $Y = \hat\psi(\hat b, A)$ be the agent's outcome prediction from an approximate belief $\hat b$ on a space $\Ycal$ with reference $\rho$ such that $\E_\pi[\delta_Y] \ll \rho$ and $H_\rho(Y)$ is finite, $\forall \pi$, consistent in that $Z \mid Y \sim \iota(Y)$ for a fixed $\iota\colon \Ycal \to \Delta(\Zcal)$.
The criterion on the law $Z \sim \psi_\# \occ$ decomposes as
\begin{equation}
  \inf_{\pi \in \Pi} H_\nu(Z), \quad \mathrm{where} \quad H_\nu(Z) = \underbrace{H_\rho(Y)}_{\text{predictive}} + \underbrace{H_\lambda(Z \mid Y)}_{\text{noise}} + \underbrace{\E\big[\ln \tfrac{\dd\nu}{\dd\lambda}(Z)\big]}_{\text{goal}} - \underbrace{H_\rho(Y \mid Z)}_{\text{ambiguity}},
  \label{eq:classes}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
\end{equation}
where every term is a functional of the $Y$ law.
The infimum over $\Pi$ is attained by a deterministic policy in $\Pi_b$ when the belief is closed and sufficient, and finite-valued for the long-run occupancy.
\end{theorem}

The theorem implies sufficient conditions on the agent's affordances, since an overly strong coarse-graining can shift the regulator below requisite variety, which may force it to randomise \citep{Singh-learning94}.
It also ties back to the notion of modelling, since by Definition~\ref{def:affordances} the representation $\hat b$, $\hat\psi$, $\iota$ and $\hat P$ is itself an affordance when viewing learning as another regulator within the same agent, whose actions modulate the primary representation.
We have not stated what the requisite-variety floor is, as it depends on both the affordances and how the goal aligns on $Z$, this is made precise next.

The goal term of Eq.\eqref{eq:classes} reinterprets reward as a log volume ratio $r(z) = -\ln \tfrac{\dd\nu}{\dd\lambda}(z)$ between the goal and the base measure on $\Zcal$.
Through $\iota$ the agent experiences this alignment signal on $\Ycal$ as $r_\Ycal(y) = \E_{Z \sim \iota(y)}[r(Z)]$, the reward it expects under the outcome law it predicts.
By consistency the goal term is $\E\big[\ln \tfrac{\dd\nu}{\dd\lambda}(Z)\big] = -\E[r_\Ycal(Y)]$, which shows that the signal the agent experiences on $\Ycal$ aligns with the exact goal term on $\Zcal$.
Rescaling $r_\Ycal$ by a factor $\beta$ gives the following proposition.
\begin{proposition}[Goal alignment]\label{prop:alignment}
  Let $\nu$ and $\lambda$ be such that $\dd\nu / \dd\lambda > 0$ exists, and let $r = -\ln \tfrac{\dd\nu}{\dd\lambda}$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Let $\nu_\beta$ for $\beta > 0$ scale the log volume of $\nu$ relative to $\lambda$, $\dd\nu_\beta / \dd\lambda = (\dd\nu / \dd\lambda)^{\beta}$.
    From Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion}, the change of base and consistency give $H_{\nu_\beta}(Z) = H_\lambda(Z) - \beta\, \E[r(Z)] = H_\lambda(Z) - \beta\, \E[r_\Ycal(Y)]$.
    \item For finite $\Scal$, $\Acal$, $\Zcal$ and bounded $\ln \tfrac{\dd\nu}{\dd\lambda}$ there is a $\beta_0$ such that $\forall \beta > \beta_0$ the deterministic minimisers of $H_{\nu_\beta}(Z)$ first minimise $\E\big[\ln \tfrac{\dd\nu}{\dd\lambda}(Z)\big]$ and $H_\lambda(Z)$ then orders the equally good solutions.
  \end{enumerate}
  For Conant and Ashby's good set, let $\dd\nu / \dd\lambda = e^{-1}$ on $G$ and $1$ on $G^c$.
  This gives $\E\big[\ln \tfrac{\dd\nu}{\dd\lambda}(Z)\big] = -\Pr(Z \in G)$, which on $\Ycal$ gives the reward $r_\Ycal(y) = \iota(y)(G)$, the predicted probability that $Z \in G$.
  So for a sufficiently large $\beta < \infty$ the minimisers first maximise $\Pr(Z \in G)$ and are then ordered by $H_\lambda(Z)$, which for feasible $G$ aligns with the criterion of \citet[p.~91]{Conant-good70}.
\end{proposition}
Prop.~\ref{prop:alignment} includes the good set of \citet{Conant-good70} in the entropy criterion through the goal measure, and the agent pursues this criterion on its own space through $r_\Ycal$.
This differs from the original work, which keeps the good set as an implicit requirement next to the entropy criterion.
The two framings do not conflict, since the goal measure only shifts their criterion to a finer space.
The next proposition states this as a pushforward from a fine space onto an observed outcome space $\Zcal$.
\begin{proposition}[Reward through coarse-graining]\label{prop:hidden-volume}
  Let $\nu$, $\lambda$ and $r$ be as in Proposition~\ref{prop:alignment}.
  For every $r$ there exists a fine space $(\Omega, \mu)$ with outcome map $J\colon \Omega \to \Zcal$ such that $\nu = J_\#\mu$, and $H_\nu(Z)$ is the entropy of the fine state against $\mu$ whenever its law given $Z$ is $\mu$ on $J^{-1}(Z)$ under every $\pi$.
\end{proposition}
Our Prop.~\ref{prop:hidden-volume} says that the environment can carry the structure that aligns the agent to a reward, provided a disturbance causes the fine state and the agent influences it only through the outcome.
This is a precise formulation of the premise behind SMiRL \citep{Berseth-smirl21}.
This echoes the consistency of Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion} at the level of the environment, where we require a fixed law of the fine state within each outcome, similar to how $\iota$ is a fixed law of the outcome within each prediction.
In both cases, the coarse-graining translates into a change of measure, which for the outcome accumulates the volume of the underlying fine states into the outcome measure and its corresponding goal.
For the prediction, the outcomes accumulate similarly into the noise and goal terms of Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion}.
Note that both conditions also fail similarly, as an agent that can choose the fine states behind an outcome or the outcomes behind a prediction can lower the entropy of the underlying law while the law of the outcome or the prediction stays trivially unaffected.

Figure~\ref{fig:lamps} gives an example of how a coarse-graining expresses a reward.
The environment is an MDP over six lamps, of which one to three are always lit.
The state $S$ is one of 41 combinations of lit lamps, and the agent's action is a binary choice to keep the current combination or reshuffle the state for a new one.
Similarly, the disturbance $D$ redraws the combination uniformly at random at every step with probability $\eta$.
The outcome $Z$ is the colour that the mixture of light blends into.
The criterion minimises $H_\nu(Z) = H_\lambda(Z) - \E[r(Z)]$, the entropy of the colour law minus the expected reward $r(z) = -\ln \tfrac{\dd\nu}{\dd\lambda}(z)$ of Prop.~\ref{prop:alignment}.
The disturbance pushes the agent towards the common colours, while the reward pushes it towards the rarer, more rewarding ones.
\add{Panel (a) illustrates the counting measure over the combinations that produce each colour, which gives the reward $r(z) = -\ln \tfrac{\dd\nu}{\dd\lambda}(z)$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:alignment} as $0$ for a primary, $-\ln 5$ for a secondary and $-\ln 23$ for white, so a colour is rewarded for how rare the disturbance makes it.}
\add{Panel (b) shows that the agent observes only whether the colour is a primary, a secondary or white, so its prediction $Y$ is this class and $\iota$ spreads it evenly over the colours of the class, which stays fixed since neither action can favour a colour within a class.}
\add{Panel (c) combines both terms, where behind each class lie the colours the agent cannot tell apart times the combinations behind each colour, which gives 3 for the primaries, 15 for the secondaries and 23 for white.}
\add{The agent therefore keeps the rare primaries where plain entropy would keep white, and as $\eta$ grows it settles for the secondaries and then for any colour but white, since a rare colour that is lost takes long to find again.}
\add{We keep the affordances deliberately simple, with two actions and an observation of the class, where an agent that observes the colour itself could keep a single primary.}

\add{The disturbance $D$ is what the regulator counters, yet it meets $D$ only through the state and its history.}
\add{What the belief can recover of $D$ the policy can counter, and what no history reveals stays as the residual entropy of \citet{Conant-good70}.}
\add{Error control is thus cause control on the belief, even a coarse one, where the agent does the best it can over the policies its model can express.}
\add{The requisite-variety floor is where this stops, as a model that merges outcomes the actions can still choose among loses its fixed $\iota$, which is the failure of consistency noted above.}
\add{Above the floor, a miscalibrated $\iota$ adds the gap $\E[\KL(p(Z \mid Y) \,\|\, \iota(Y))]$ to the noise term.}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!4, colframe=gray!60, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, left=6pt, right=6pt, top=4pt, bottom=4pt]
\small
\textbf{Takeaway on the Recipe.}
The good regulator frames an RL objective as minimizing the entropy of an outcome law in Eq.\eqref{eq:classes}.
The measure of this entropy must encode goal-seeking behavior, and the affordances of the agent determine how the outcome law is influenced, represented, and coarse-grained.
The law of requisite variety and the agent's affordances are what lead to the necessary modelling criterion of \citet{Conant-good70}, since unmodelled aspects of the regulation problem within the agent's affordances show up as residual noise.
An optimal policy is deterministic for an agent with a sufficient belief, whereas for an insufficient agent, whose affordances sit below the requisite variety, it may have to randomize.
\end{tcolorbox}
